06 Plain-English guide to every number on this page
VIX

The stock market's 'fear gauge.' It shows how much traders expect the S&P 500 to move, up or down, over the next month, based on what they're paying for options right now. It doesn't say which direction, only how big the swings are expected to be.

What it tells youThink of it as the price tag on what you sell. When it's low, buyers pay little for protection, so selling puts earns little. 15 to 20 is the normal, comfortable range. 20 to 28 pays better, but prices swing enough to reach your strike more often. Above 28 the pay looks great, but that's usually because something frightening is happening, and it's exactly when a sold put is most likely to leave you owning the stock.

Counted in the stress score?Yes. Under 15: 0 points. 15 to 20: 1 point. 20 to 28: 2 points. 28 or more: 3 points.

VIX vs 3-month

This month's fear gauge (VIX) divided by the same gauge measured over three months (VIX3M). Normally the three-month number is a little higher, because the further ahead you look, the less certain things are, so the ratio usually sits below 1.00.

What it tells youOne of the most useful numbers on the page. When the ratio climbs above 1.00, traders are more worried about the next few weeks than about the next few months. That's what real stress looks like, and it often shows up here before the VIX itself looks alarming.

Counted in the stress score?Yes. Below 0.90: 0 points. 0.90 to 1.00: 1 point. Above 1.00: 2 points.

MOVE

The bond market's fear gauge: how much traders expect US government interest rates to swing over the next month.

What it tells youInterest rates are the foundation every other price is built on, so trouble often shows up here before it reaches stocks. Under 95 is calm. 95 to 120 means the bond market is jumpy and stocks may follow. Above 120, interest rates are driving the whole market, so be extra careful around Fed meetings and inflation reports.

Counted in the stress score?Partly. Under 95: 0 points. 95 to 120: listed as a watch item but no points. 120 or more: 1 point.

10-year rate

The interest rate the US government pays to borrow money for 10 years. It's the most-watched interest rate in the world, and mortgage rates and company borrowing costs follow it.

What it tells youInvestors use this rate to work out what a company's future profits are worth today. When it rises quickly, shares of fast-growing companies (tech especially) usually fall, even if nothing about the business has changed. How fast it moves matters more than the level.

Counted in the stress score?No. It's used to build the yield-curve number below.

Yield curve

The 10-year government interest rate minus the 2-year rate, in percentage points. Lending for 10 years normally pays more than lending for 2, so this is usually positive. The 2-year rate comes from the Federal Reserve's free FRED database; if that doesn't load, the 5-year rate is used instead and the tile says so.

What it tells youPositive and widening is the normal, healthy shape. Shrinking toward zero suggests the economy is later in its cycle. Below zero ('upside down' or 'inverted') means investors expect the Fed to cut rates later, and in the past that has often come a year or more before a slowdown. On its own it isn't a reason to stop selling puts; it matters more when junk bonds are also falling and the fear gauges are rising.

Counted in the stress score?Yes. 1 point when it's below zero. 0 otherwise.

Inflation (CPI)

How much prices overall have risen over the past 12 months, measured by the government's Consumer Price Index. It's the number the Federal Reserve watches most closely. It only updates once a month.

What it tells youThink of inflation as the cause and most of this page as the effect. When inflation is high or rising, the Fed keeps interest rates high or raises them, which makes borrowing more expensive and weighs on stock prices. A reading that's rising again is called out separately, because that kind of surprise moves markets most.

Counted in the stress score?Yes. Under 2.5%: 0 points. 2.5% to 3.5%: 1 point. 3.5% or more: 2 points.

Junk bonds (HYG)

A fund that holds bonds from riskier, lower-rated companies (often called junk bonds). Its price change over the last five days is used as a quick read on whether lenders are getting nervous.

What it tells youIf a company gets into trouble, its lenders are paid before its shareholders, so lenders tend to notice problems first. If this fund has been falling for a week, lenders are pulling back, often before stocks react. If it's steady while the VIX is high, the stock market's nerves are probably about trading and positioning, not companies actually going bust.

Counted in the stress score?Yes. 1 point if it has fallen more than 1% over five days.

US dollar

The US Dollar Index (DXY): the dollar's value against a basket of major currencies, mostly the euro.

What it tells youA fast-rising dollar makes borrowing in dollars harder around the world and cuts the value of US companies' overseas sales, so it usually goes with nervous markets. A falling dollar tends to go with calmer markets and higher commodity prices. How fast it moves matters more than the level.

Counted in the stress score?No. Background only.

Oil (WTI)

The price of US crude oil, in dollars per barrel.

What it tells youOil is one of the fastest ways world events turn into inflation. A sudden jump is a risk in itself and a reason for the Fed to keep rates higher. A sudden drop usually means traders expect a slower economy. Read it alongside the Energy & Oil headlines further down.

Counted in the stress score?No. Background only.

Oil (Brent)

The price of international crude oil (the kind shipped by sea), in dollars per barrel. It's the oil price most affected by the Middle East.

What it tells youReads like US oil above. When Brent pulls well ahead of US oil, it usually points to a shipping or supply-route problem, like the Strait of Hormuz, rather than a change in how much oil the world wants. In panel 02, Brent's price and its move over the last five trading days feed the scenario light.

Counted in the stress score?Not in the stress score. It is one of the scenario light's inputs in panel 02.

Gold

The price of gold, in dollars per ounce.

What it tells youGold usually falls when interest rates rise, because it pays no interest. The telling case is when that breaks down: if gold is rising at the same time as interest rates and the dollar, investors are usually looking for safety for a reason that doesn't show up anywhere else on this page yet.

Counted in the stress score?No. Background only.

Stocks vs economy

Often called the Buffett Indicator: the total value of the US stock market divided by the size of the whole economy (GDP). Calculated from two free Federal Reserve data series.

What it tells youAbove 100% means the stock market is worth more than a full year of everything the US economy produces. Today's reading is a record. It moves slowly and is a poor timing tool, since it can stay high for years, but very high readings have raised the odds of an eventual pullback in the past.

Counted in the stress score?No. It only updates a few times a year.

Fed's key rate

The interest rate the Federal Reserve sets for overnight lending between banks: the Fed's main tool for speeding up or slowing down the economy. Shown as the Fed's official target range, straight from the Fed's own data.

What it tells youThe level tells you where the Fed is today. What matters more is what comes next, and panel 02 shows that each morning: how many more hikes or cuts the market expects, and whether that changed this week.

Counted in the stress score?No. Updated daily.

Fed hikes expected

How many more quarter-point rate hikes (or cuts) the market expects from the Fed over the next 12 months. It's worked out from the 1-year Treasury bill: that bill's rate is roughly the average interest rate investors expect over the coming year, so the gap between it and the Fed's current rate shows how far they expect the Fed to move. Every 0.25 points of gap is about one quarter-point hike.

What it tells youA free way to see the market's Fed forecast every morning, without paying for futures data. The number tells you how many moves are expected; the change from last week tells you whether the market now expects more or fewer. Treat tiny changes (a tenth of a hike or less) as noise.

Counted in the stress score?No. It feeds the plain-English summary in panel 02.

Rate expected in 6–12 mo

The short-term interest rate the market expects for the period six to twelve months from now. It's worked out from today's 6-month and 1-year bill rates: if the 1-year bill pays more than the 6-month bill, investors must expect rates to be higher in the second half of the year.

What it tells youIt focuses on next spring and summer, which is when the question 'one more hike, or a pause?' gets settled. A rise of 0.10 points or more in a week means the market has moved noticeably toward more hikes. The math is a simple approximation, accurate to a few hundredths of a point, which is plenty for seeing the direction.

Counted in the stress score?Not in the stress score. A 0.10-point move in a week is one of the scenario light's signals.

1-yr rate a year out

The 1-year interest rate the market expects a year from today, worked out from today's 1-year and 2-year rates.

What it tells youIt answers a practical question: should I buy a 2-year note now, or a 1-year bill now and another one next year? If you think 1-year rates a year from now will be below this number, the 2-year note is the better buy. If you think they'll be above it, buying 1-year bills twice wins.

Counted in the stress score?No. A reference for buying Treasuries.

Bill vs your cash

The 1-year Treasury bill rate minus what your money-market fund pays. The money-market rate is typed in by hand (CASH_BENCHMARK at the top of the script), because no free source publishes it.

What it tells youHow much more you'd earn in a year by moving idle cash into a 1-year bill and holding it. Money-market rates follow the Fed, so if more hikes come, the real gap over the year will end up a little smaller than today's number.

Counted in the stress score?No. A cash-management reference.

Gasoline

Wholesale gasoline futures, in dollars per gallon. Not the pump price, but pump prices follow it within a few weeks.

What it tells youGasoline is how an oil shock reaches households fastest, and it shapes what people expect inflation to be. A lasting rise here makes the long-run inflation number more likely to move.

Counted in the stress score?No. Background only.

Inflation expected, 5 yrs

The average yearly inflation investors expect over the next five years. It's the gap between the regular 5-year Treasury rate and the rate on the 5-year inflation-protected Treasury (TIPS). From the Fed's FRED data, series T5YIE.

What it tells youThis rises and falls with oil prices, so it going up during an oil spike is expected. On its own it doesn't tell you whether the spike will last; the long-run number next to it answers that.

Counted in the stress score?No.

Inflation expected, 10 yrs

The same measure over ten years (FRED series T10YIE).

What it tells youMoves like the 5-year version but less, because more of the ten years lies beyond today's oil shock.

Counted in the stress score?No.

Long-run inflation expected

The yearly inflation investors expect for the five years that start five years from now (FRED series T5YIFR, known as the '5-year, 5-year forward'). It deliberately ignores what's happening today and asks what inflation will look like once today's shock has passed.

What it tells youThis is the Fed's favorite test of whether an oil shock is turning into lasting inflation. If oil jumps and this barely moves, investors believe the shock is temporary, and the Fed can stop raising rates soon. If it climbs by 0.10 points or more in a month, the shock is starting to stick, which tends to push long-term rates up and bring more rate hikes.

Counted in the stress score?Not in the stress score. Its one-month change is one of the scenario light's signals.

Extra pay to lend long

The extra interest investors demand for lending to the government for 10 years, compared with lending for a few months at a time and rolling it over. Economists call it the term premium. This is the Federal Reserve Board's estimate (FRED series THREEFYTP10), which arrives about a week late. News stories often quote the New York Fed's estimate instead; it gives a different number but usually moves the same way.

What it tells youWhen long-term rates rise because of worries about government debt, heavy government borrowing, or foreign buyers stepping back, rather than because of the Fed, it shows up here. If this is rising while Fed expectations hold steady, debt worries are pushing long-term rates up, and Fed rate cuts wouldn't fix that.

Counted in the stress score?No. The direction over a month matters more than the level.

Scenario light (panel 02)

Sorts each morning into one of three stories. Base: the war drags on, oil stays high, and the Fed raises rates once more and then pauses. De-escalation: a deal, oil falls, and the market drops the rate hikes it expected. Escalation: wider disruption, inflation expectations climb, and more rate hikes. The light counts simple signals for each side: oil's price and its move over five trading days, the one-month change in long-run inflation expectations, the one-week change in the rate expected in 6 to 12 months, and the one-week change in the 10-year rate. Three or more signals on one side, and more than on the other, switch the light; otherwise it stays on Base. The trigger levels live in SCENARIO_THRESHOLDS at the top of the script. It is separate from the stress score at the top of the page: the stress score asks 'should I sell options today?', the scenario light asks 'which story is the bond market telling?'

Government interest rates table and chart (panel 02)

The interest rates the US government pays to borrow for different lengths of time, from 1 month to 30 years. These are the official figures the Treasury publishes each afternoon, so they run up to a day behind the live market. The change columns show how much each rate moved since the previous day, about a week ago, and about a month ago, in percentage points: a move from 4.50% to 4.75% is +0.25. The chart compares today with a month ago. If the short end (left side) rose most, expectations for the Fed changed. If the long end (right side) rose most, worries about inflation or government debt grew.

Rate gaps (panel 02)

Three differences between long- and short-term rates: 10-year minus 3-month, 10-year minus 2-year, and 30-year minus 5-year. When a gap widens because long-term rates are rising, that's usually worry about debt and inflation. When it widens because short-term rates are falling, the market is expecting rate cuts.

Government debt auctions (panel 02)

The government borrows by auctioning new notes and bonds several times a month. For the latest auction of each length, the table shows the interest rate the auction set and two measures of demand, each compared with the usual level (the average of the previous six auctions of the same length). 'Bids per $1 sold' is how many dollars investors offered for each dollar of debt sold; lower means fewer buyers. 'Left with dealers' is the share the big Wall Street banks ended up holding; they're required to bid, so a bigger share means other buyers stayed away. Weak auctions push long-term rates up. One common measure, the 'tail', isn't shown because no free source has the data needed to calculate it.

Central-bank countdown (panel 02)

Days until each upcoming interest-rate decision by the US Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, the Bank of Japan and the Bank of England, plus other key dates. Anything within five days turns amber. Only officially announced dates are listed; when a bank runs out of listed dates, the panel reminds you to add its next ones.

Stock markets around the world (panel 03)

Stock markets in Asia and Europe, plus where US stock futures are pointing, each compared with its last close. By the time the US market opens, moves that already happened overseas are usually 'priced in'. A move in US futures that Asia and Europe didn't also make is more likely to fade once regular trading starts.

Currencies (panel 03)

Major currencies against the US dollar. USD/JPY is the one to watch most closely. A sudden jump in the Japanese yen (which shows up as USD/JPY dropping sharply) has in the past been an early sign that big funds are unwinding trades built on borrowing cheap yen, and that kind of unwinding can hit stock prices faster than the headlines suggest.

Sector and industry performance (panel 04)

How each part of the US stock market has done so far this year, over the past two years, and over the past three years. Each group is tracked through a fund (ETF) that holds its stocks: the eleven SPDR sector funds for sectors, and narrower funds for industries such as semiconductors or regional banks. The figures include dividends and are totals, not yearly averages: +40% over 2 years means $100 became $140. Hover over a 2-year or 3-year figure to see the yearly equivalent and how far it is ahead of or behind the S&P 500. Use it to see where the market's leadership has been, not to predict what comes next. A dash means there wasn't enough price history, and the brief never fills a gap with a guess. Click a column heading to re-sort.

Buying and selling pressure tags (panel 04)

A small 'in' or 'out' badge next to a sector or industry, shown only for the ones that stand out most from the rest. This is NOT real fund-flow data. Actual money flows into and out of funds aren't available from any free source this brief uses. Instead it's an estimate built from ordinary daily trading: on each day, trading volume counts as buying when the price closed near the day's high and as selling when it closed near the day's low, averaged over the past six months. It can be thrown off by thin holiday trading or one unusually large trade, so treat a tag as 'worth a second look', not as proof that money is moving.

Stress score (top of page)

Adds up the points from the scored numbers in panel 01, out of 10. It isn't a prediction. It shows how many separate, unrelated signals agree that something is off. 0 to 2 (green): the gauges are calm and mostly agree. 3 to 5 (amber): they disagree with each other, a good time to trade smaller. 6 or more (red): several unrelated measures are stressed at once, and that kind of agreement across different corners of the market is worth taking seriously, more than any single number being high.
